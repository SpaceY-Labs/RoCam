\title{Robust Small Rocket Detection with YOLO26s:\\
NMS-Free Edge Deployment, Aggressive Augmentation,\\
and Comprehensive Ablation Study}
\author{\authname}
\date{April 2026}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
  We present the complete training methodology, deployment pipeline, and
  experimental validation for a small-rocket detection system designed for
  real-time edge inference on the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano.  Using
  \textsc{Yolo26s} (9.47M parameters, stride $\{8, 16, 32\}$) with its
  \textbf{NMS-free one-to-one detection head}, we train with aggressive
  data augmentation centred on \textbf{full mosaic} ($\text{mosaic}=1.0$),
  multi-scale input ($544$--$960$\,px), and 7~custom Albumentations
  transforms targeting real-world imaging degradation.  The model is
  trained for 420 epochs on 15{,}832 images (including 4{,}000 COCO hard
  negatives), achieving validation
  $\text{mAP}_{50\text{-}95} = 0.694$.  After ONNX export and
  TensorRT FP16 compilation, the deployed model achieves
  \textbf{DeepStream $\text{mAP}_{50\text{-}95} = 0.631$},
  $\text{mAP}_{50} = 0.875$, and
  $\text{mAP}_{50\text{-}95}(\text{small}) = 0.341$ on the Jetson Orin
  Nano at $540{\times}960$ input resolution.

  We additionally conduct a \textbf{comprehensive ablation study spanning
  10~experimental plans} (Plans~A through~J), investigating the P2
  stride-4 architecture, varied mosaic probabilities, tiled (SAHI)
  training, copy-paste augmentation, and fine-tuning versus
  from-scratch strategies.  The P2 variant, despite achieving
  superior training metrics ($\text{mAP}_{50\text{-}95} = 0.765$),
  suffers catastrophic degradation under FP16 quantisation
  ($0.765 \to 0.539$, $-29.5\%$), confirming that
  \textbf{deployment-validated accuracy} must guide model selection.
  All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA H100 GPUs at McMaster
  University's Grace HPC cluster.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ====================================================================

Detecting small, fast-moving rockets in surveillance footage poses unique
challenges for modern object detectors.  The targets of interest typically
occupy fewer than $30{\times}30$ pixels in $1920{\times}1080$ frames, can
appear at \emph{any} orientation ($0$--$360^{\circ}$), and are subject to
imaging degradation including motion blur, compression artefacts, and
varying illumination.

The \progname{} system requires \textbf{real-time inference on edge
hardware}---specifically the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano (8\,GB), running a
full GPU pipeline: CSI camera capture at $1920{\times}1080$@60\,fps,
TensorRT FP16 inference at $540{\times}960$, proportional gimbal tracking,
and GL shader live zoom---all without CPU-bound post-processing.  This
deployment constraint introduces a critical gap between training-time and
deployment-time accuracy: models that excel in FP32 validation may degrade
significantly under FP16 quantisation.

We select \textsc{Yolo26s} (9.47M parameters, stride $\{8, 16, 32\}$) as
our architecture, leveraging its \textbf{NMS-free one-to-one detection
head} that eliminates non-maximum suppression entirely, reducing latency
and avoiding duplicate-suppression errors on the edge.

\noindent Our contributions include:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{NMS-free edge deployment}: documentation and validation
        of the one-to-one detection head with DeepStream
        \texttt{cluster-mode=4}, eliminating NMS post-processing.
  \item \textbf{Deployment-validated training}: end-to-end pipeline from
        H100 training to FP16 benchmark on Jetson Orin Nano, with
        deployment metrics as the primary selection criterion.
  \item \textbf{Full mosaic augmentation}: demonstration that
        $\text{mosaic} = 1.0$ is critical for small-object detection,
        with all reduced-mosaic variants failing (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
  \item \textbf{Comprehensive ablation study}: 10~experimental plans
        spanning architecture variants, augmentation strategies,
        fine-tuning versus from-scratch training, and tiled inference.
  \item \textbf{P2 failure analysis}: the stride-4 detection head
        achieves $+10\%$ training mAP but suffers $-30\%$ deployment
        degradation under FP16 quantisation.
  \item \textbf{Model evolution history}: progression from YOLO11s
        baseline through 5~YOLO26s iterations to the deployed model.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{COCO Hard Negative Mining}
\label{sec:coco_neg}

To suppress false positives, we inject \textbf{4{,}000 pure background
images} from MS~COCO~\citep{lin2014coco} into the training set.  Images
are randomly sampled (seed$=0$) from COCO train2017 and val2017
(${\sim}123$K total), copied into the training directory with empty label
files to provide pure background supervision.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
  \caption{COCO Hard Negative Mining Pipeline}
  \label{alg:coco}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require COCO train2017 + val2017 images (${\sim}123$K total)
    \Require Target count $N = 4{,}000$
    \State Download and extract COCO images (cached after first run)
    \State $S \gets \textsc{RandomSample}(\text{all COCO images}, N, \text{seed}=0)$
    \For{each image $s_i \in S$}
    \State Copy $s_i$ to \texttt{images/train/coco\_neg\_\{i\}.jpg}
    \State Create empty label file \texttt{labels/train/coco\_neg\_\{i\}.txt}
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

At 25.3\% of the training set, the COCO negatives force a harder decision
boundary critical for suppressing false positives during deployment.

\subsection{YOLO26s}

YOLO26~\citep{ultralytics2025yolo} is the latest generation of the
Ultralytics YOLO family, featuring an updated backbone and a
\textbf{dual-head design}: a one-to-many (O2M) head used during training
for higher-recall gradient supervision, and a one-to-one (O2O) head used
for end-to-end inference without NMS.

    \midrule

    Layers                    & 454                     \\
    Detection strides         & $\{8, 16, 32\}$         \\
    Detection heads           & 3 (O2O) + 3 (O2M)      \\
    Training head             & One-to-many (O2M)       \\
    Inference head            & One-to-one (O2O)        \\
    Pretrained weights        & COCO (\texttt{yolo26s.pt}) \\

  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{NMS-Free One-to-One Detection Head}
\label{sec:nms_free}

A key architectural advantage of YOLO26 for edge deployment is its
\textbf{NMS-free inference pathway}.  During ONNX export for DeepStream,
only the one-to-one (O2O) detection head is exported via a custom
\texttt{forward\_deepstream} function
(\texttt{convert\_tensorrt.py}, lines~49--65).
This head produces at most one detection per spatial anchor, eliminating
the need for NMS post-processing entirely.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \fbox{\parbox{0.92\textwidth}{\small\ttfamily
    \textbf{forward\_deepstream(self, x):} \\
    \quad x\_detach = [xi.detach() for xi in x] \\
    \quad one2one = [cat(self.one2one\_cv2[i](x), \\
    \quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad self.one2one\_cv3[i](x), dim=1) \\
    \quad\quad\quad\quad\quad for i in range(self.nl)] \\
    \quad return self.inference(one2one)
  }}
  \caption{Simplified ONNX export forward function.  Only the O2O head
    branches (\texttt{one2one\_cv2}, \texttt{one2one\_cv3}) are exported;
    the O2M training head is discarded.}
  \label{lst:forward_ds}
\end{figure}

On the DeepStream side, the nvinfer plugin is configured with
\texttt{cluster-mode=4}, which is specifically designed for NMS-free
architectures (YOLOv10, RT-DETR, and one-to-one heads).  This means:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \texttt{nms-iou-threshold=0.45} in the pgie configuration is
        \textbf{not applied}---it is a legacy value that has no effect
        when \texttt{cluster-mode=4} is active.
  \item \texttt{pre-cluster-threshold=0.25} performs \textbf{confidence
        filtering only}---detections below this threshold are discarded.
  \item No duplicate suppression occurs; the O2O head inherently produces
        non-overlapping detections.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This architecture eliminates NMS latency and avoids the risk of
NMS incorrectly suppressing valid detections, which is particularly
important for edge deployment where every millisecond counts.

\section{Data Augmentation Strategy}
\label{sec:augmentation}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{The Critical Role of Full Mosaic}
\label{sec:mosaic}

The single most important augmentation decision is setting
$\texttt{mosaic} = 1.0$, meaning \textbf{every training image} is
composed as a $2{\times}2$ mosaic of four random images.  This produces
three key effects:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Object density}: Each mosaic tile contains a full image
        scaled to $\frac{1}{4}$ area, quadrupling the effective object
        density per training sample.
  \item \textbf{Scale diversity}: The $\frac{1}{4}$-scale mosaic tiles
        create additional small-object examples, directly augmenting the
        under-represented COCO-small category.
  \item \textbf{Context mixing}: Objects appear against varied backgrounds
        from different images, improving generalisation.
\end{enumerate}

Mosaic is disabled for the final 80 epochs (\texttt{close\_mosaic=80}) to
allow the model to refine on full-resolution images.

\subsection{Model Input Resolution}
\label{sec:deploy_resolution}

The deployment input resolution is \textbf{$540{\times}960$}, computed as:
\[
  \text{height}_\text{deploy} = \frac{1080}{2} = 540, \quad
  \text{width}_\text{deploy} = \frac{1920}{2} = 960
\]
This is hard-coded in \texttt{convert\_tensorrt.py} (lines~35--36).  The
training resolution is $544{\times}960$; the 4-pixel height difference is
absorbed by DeepStream's letterbox padding
(\texttt{maintain-aspect-ratio=1}, \texttt{symmetric-padding=1}).

\subsection{Export and Compilation}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{ONNX export} (on Grace/H100): The trained
        \texttt{best.pt} is exported to ONNX with the
        \texttt{forward\_deepstream} monkey-patch.  A
        \texttt{DeepStreamOutput} layer concatenates
        $[\text{boxes}, \text{scores}, \text{labels}]$ into a single
        output tensor.  Export must be performed on Grace due to
        numpy~2.x PCG64 random-state compatibility.
  \item \textbf{Transfer}: The ONNX model is transferred to Orin via
        \texttt{scp}.
  \item \textbf{TensorRT compilation} (on Orin): The ONNX model is
        compiled to a TensorRT engine with \textbf{FP16 precision}.
        Engines are cached by MD5 hash in
        \texttt{/mnt/data/tensorrt\_engine\_cache}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{FP16 Quantisation Considerations}

TensorRT FP16 quantisation reduces weights and activations from FP32 to
FP16, halving memory bandwidth and enabling Tensor Core acceleration.
However, FP16's reduced dynamic range (5-bit exponent vs.\ 8-bit in FP32)
can cause accuracy degradation, particularly in architectures with
high-resolution feature maps (e.g., stride-4 heads producing
$240{\times}240$ feature maps).  The standard \textsc{Yolo26s} (minimum
stride$=8$) is robust to FP16, with a manageable $-9.1\%$ accuracy gap.

\section{Benchmark Methodology}
\label{sec:benchmark}
%% ====================================================================

All deployment metrics are measured using a custom
\texttt{accuracy\_benchmark.py} script that evaluates the DeepStream
pipeline's output against ground-truth annotations.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Image staging}: Original images are letterbox-padded
        (not resized) to $1920{\times}1080$ to match the camera's native
        resolution.  Aspect ratio is preserved with symmetric black
        padding.
  \item \textbf{Ground truth conversion}: YOLO-format normalised
        annotations are transformed to staged pixel coordinates,
        accounting for the letterbox offset.
  \item \textbf{DeepStream inference}: Each staged image is pushed through
        the full DeepStream pipeline (nvinfer with TensorRT FP16,
        \texttt{cluster-mode=4}, \texttt{pre-cluster-threshold=0.25}).
  \item \textbf{Single detection selection}: For each image, only the
        highest-confidence detection is retained.  This matches the
        production pipeline where one rocket per frame is expected.
  \item \textbf{COCO-style evaluation}: Detections and ground truth are
        evaluated using \texttt{pycocotools.COCOeval}, producing mAP at
        IoU thresholds 0.50--0.95.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Size-Based Metrics}

COCO evaluation reports mAP by object size:
\textbf{small} ($\text{area} < 32^2$\,px$^2$),
\textbf{medium} ($32^2 \leq \text{area} < 96^2$),
\textbf{large} ($\text{area} \geq 96^2$).
The \textbf{DS mAP$_{50\text{-}95}$(small)} metric is our primary
decision criterion.

\subsection{Root Cause: FP16 Quantisation Sensitivity}

The stride-4 head operates on $240{\times}240$ feature maps, producing
$16{\times}$ more spatial positions than stride-8.  FP16's reduced
precision causes:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Spatial precision loss}: Fine-grained positional encoding
        is quantised away.
  \item \textbf{Activation range compression}: Wider dynamic range in
        high-res features cannot be faithfully represented.
  \item \textbf{Accumulated rounding}: More layers at high resolution
        means more sequential rounding errors.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Lesson.}  Architecture selection for edge deployment must be
validated \textbf{end-to-end at the target precision}.

pipeline, and comprehensive experimental validation for a small-rocket
detection system achieving
DS~mAP$_{50\text{-}95} = \mathbf{0.631}$ and
DS~mAP$_{50\text{-}95}(\text{small}) = \mathbf{0.341}$ on the Jetson Orin
Nano with TensorRT FP16 at $540{\times}960$.  The key insights are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{NMS-free deployment eliminates post-processing latency.}
        YOLO26's one-to-one detection head, combined with DeepStream's
        \texttt{cluster-mode=4}, produces non-overlapping detections
        without NMS.  The \texttt{nms-iou-threshold} config parameter
        is not applied.

  \item \textbf{Full mosaic is non-negotiable for small objects.}
        $\text{mosaic} = 1.0$ with $\texttt{close\_mosaic} = 80$
        provides the best augmentation balance.  All reduced-mosaic
        variants ($0.80$, $0.30$) underperformed by $-20\%$ or more.

  \item \textbf{Deployment metrics must drive model selection.}  The P2
        architecture achieves $+10.2\%$ higher training mAP but $-14.6\%$
        \emph{lower} deployment mAP due to FP16 quantisation.  Across
        10~plans, training metrics were unreliable predictors of
        deployment performance.

  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning regresses small-object detection.}
        Three fine-tuning experiments consistently improved overall mAP
        while degrading small-object mAP by $8$--$17\%$.  From-scratch
        training with 420 epochs and full mosaic is superior.

  \item \textbf{The quantisation gap is architecture-dependent.}
        \textsc{Yolo26s} loses $9.1\%$ under FP16; \textsc{Yolo26s-P2}
        loses $29.5\%$.  High-resolution feature maps (stride-4) are
        disproportionately affected.

  \item \textbf{COCO hard negatives suppress false positives.}
        4{,}000 pure background images (25\%) force a harder decision
        boundary critical for real-world deployment.

  \item \textbf{Multi-scale training improves robustness.}  Variable
        resolution ($544$--$960$\,px) produces scale-invariant features
        for the extreme size range in our dataset.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Three additional experiments (Plans~H, I, J) completed training
and await deployment benchmark.  Plan~I (tiled/SAHI training with
50{,}741 mixed images) achieved the highest validation mAP ($0.688$) and
represents the most promising path forward.
